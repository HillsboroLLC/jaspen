### Email 2: What Your Style Looks Like In Real Life

Subject: What your decision style can look like in real life

Preview text: Your profile is not a label. It is a useful lens for noticing how you think.

Primary CTA: Explore your Decision Profile

Full email copy:

Hi there,

Your Decision Profile is not meant to put you in a box. It is meant to give you language for patterns you may already recognize.

If your result was `{decision_style_name}`, you may notice it in ordinary moments at work: how you respond when a meeting needs a direction, how you handle unclear priorities, how much information you want before making a recommendation, or how you explain a choice after it has been made.

You may also see it outside of work. It can show up when you are planning a move, choosing between financial options, deciding how to spend your time, helping someone else think through a problem, or trying to make a personal decision without overthinking it.

The useful part is not whether the profile feels perfect. The useful part is whether it helps you notice what you tend to trust first.

Every style has strengths. Some people move quickly and create momentum. Some people slow the room down enough to see the facts. Some people keep others aligned. Some people hold several possibilities open before choosing. Some people look for the practical path that can actually work.

Every strength has a tradeoff too. Speed can skip assumptions. Careful analysis can wait too long. Consensus can blur ownership. Exploration can keep too many options alive. Practicality can solve for the nearest constraint instead of the real one.

That is normal. Decision-making is not about having a perfect style. It is about knowing how your style helps you and where it may need support.

Explore your Decision Profile when you want to revisit what your result may mean.

P.S. The point is not to change how you think. The point is to make your thinking easier to see.

### Email 3: A Decision This Style Often Struggles With

Subject: The kind of decision your style may find harder

Preview text: Some decisions are difficult because they pull against the way we naturally think.

Primary CTA: Revisit your Decision Profile

Full email copy:

Hi there,

Some decisions feel difficult because the stakes are high. Others feel difficult because they pull against our natural decision style.

For someone with a `{decision_style_name}` profile, one common example is choosing between two good options where neither option is clearly wrong.

That might be two job offers. It might be whether to stay in a role or start something new. It might be hiring someone who is strong in one way but risky in another. It might be buying a home, investing money, ending a project, or choosing a direction for a business.

These decisions are hard because they do not usually fail from a lack of information alone. They are hard because the tradeoffs are real.

One option may feel safer. Another may offer more growth. One may make sense on paper. Another may fit your values better. One may be easier to explain. Another may be more honest about what you actually want.

This is where decision style matters. Your natural pattern may help you see something important early. It may also pull your attention away from something equally important.

Awareness helps before anything else changes. Once you can name the tension, you can ask better questions.

What am I overvaluing because it feels familiar?

What am I avoiding because it creates discomfort?

What would I want to be able to explain six months from now?

You do not need to solve the entire decision in one sitting. Sometimes the first step is simply seeing why it feels stuck.

Revisit your Decision Profile and think about one decision in your life or work that might be harder because of the way you naturally decide.

P.S. A hard decision does not mean you are bad at deciding. It may simply mean the decision is asking for a kind of thinking your usual pattern does not cover on its own.

## Sequence 2: Decision Planning Toolkit

### Email 2: The Mistake Most People Make With Weighted Scoring

Subject: The mistake most people make with weighted scoring

Preview text: The spreadsheet is useful, but the numbers are only as good as the thinking behind them.

Primary CTA: Build a decision in Jaspen

Full email copy:

Hi there,

Weighted scoring can be genuinely useful.

It gives structure to a decision that might otherwise stay vague. It forces you to name what matters. It helps you compare options against the same criteria instead of changing the rules as you go.

The mistake is treating the final number as if it is objective truth.

A score can look precise even when the thinking underneath it is shaky.

Imagine you are choosing between two vendors. One scores higher because it looks easier to implement. Another scores lower because it seems more expensive. On paper, the first vendor wins.

But what if the implementation score is based on a weak assumption? What if the team has never used a tool like it before? What if the cheaper vendor will require more internal support than expected? What if the more expensive vendor reduces risk in a way the spreadsheet did not capture?

The spreadsheet did not lie. It just reflected the thinking that was put into it.

That is why weighted scoring works best when it is paired with clear assumptions, visible tradeoffs, and a willingness to question the inputs.

The goal is not to make the numbers disappear. The goal is to make the numbers explainable.

A good score should help you ask better questions:

Why did this option win?

Which assumption is doing the most work?

What would change the result?

Where are we pretending to know more than we do?

If you want to go beyond the worksheet, build a decision in Jaspen and let it help you examine the reasoning behind the score.

P.S. A score is useful when it starts a better conversation, not when it ends the conversation too early.

### Email 3: Why Assumptions Matter More Than Scoring

Subject: The assumptions are carrying the decision

Preview text: Most decisions depend on assumptions that never get written down.

Primary CTA: Identify assumptions in Jaspen

Full email copy:

Hi there,

Every important decision contains assumptions.

Some are obvious. We assume a cost estimate is close enough. We assume a timeline is realistic. We assume a person has the capacity to take something on. We assume customers will respond a certain way. We assume a risk is unlikely enough to accept.

Other assumptions are quieter.

We assume the current priority will still matter in six months. We assume the team understands the goal the same way. We assume an option is too expensive, too risky, too slow, or too complicated before we have actually tested that belief.

The issue is not that assumptions are bad. We cannot make decisions without them.

The issue is that most people never write them down.

When assumptions stay invisible, it becomes hard to learn from the decision later. If the decision works, you may not know which assumptions were right. If it fails, you may only remember the outcome, not the reasoning that produced it.

That makes future judgment harder.

Capturing assumptions changes the decision. It gives you something to inspect before you commit. It helps a team see where confidence is real and where confidence is borrowed from habit, pressure, or convenience.

It also gives you a better record later.

You can look back and ask: what did we believe at the time? What changed? What did we miss? What should we carry forward into the next decision?

That is where Jaspen can help. It gives you a place to name assumptions, connect them to your criteria, and preserve the reasoning around the choice.

Try running one existing decision through Jaspen and ask: what assumptions are holding this together?

P.S. Better decisions often begin when the hidden assumptions become visible.
